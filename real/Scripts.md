
## Slide 1 – Student Performance Data Analysis
*~20 seconds*

Good morning everyone.

Today, we will present our analysis of student performance, with a particular focus on what the findings may mean for the *Parents’ Association and school leaders*.

Our main goal is simple:

*How can we recognise students who may need support earlier, and what patterns should families and schools pay attention to?*

---

## Slide 2 – Problem and Research Question
*~45 seconds*

Let’s begin with why this issue matters.

In our dataset, *100 out of 649 students failed their final grade*.

That is about *1 in 7 students*.

So we wanted to understand three things.

First, *when can we identify a student who may be at risk?*

Second, *which habits are linked with higher or lower grades?*

And third, *which students may need extra support?*

One important point before we continue is that this analysis shows us relationships in the data.

It does *not* prove that one factor directly causes another.

So our goal is to identify useful warning signs and patterns, rather than claim that one action will definitely improve a student’s grade.

---

## Slide 3 – Dataset and Analysis Approach
*~40 seconds*

The dataset includes *649 students and 33 different variables*.

These include information about study habits, family background, social activities, absences and academic performance.

The students were between *15 and 22 years old*, and they came from two secondary schools in Portugal.

Our main outcome was the final grade, called G3, which ranges from zero to twenty.

We first checked the data, then explored important patterns, created visualisations, and finally used a simple statistical model to look at several factors together.

---

## Slide 4 – Final Grade Overview
*~35 seconds*

First, let’s look at the overall picture.

The average final grade was about *11.9 out of 20*, and the median was 12.

However, *100 students, or around 15%, scored below the pass mark of 10*.

So although most students passed, there was still a clear group who struggled.

This gave us a reason to look more closely at the patterns connected with lower and higher performance.

---

## Slide 5 – Study Time
*~45 seconds*

One of the clearest patterns was study time.

Students who reported studying for *less than two hours per week* had an average final grade of about *10.8*.

Students who reported studying for *five to ten hours per week* had an average of about *13.2*.

That is an observed difference of around *2.4 points*.

So in this sample, students who reported more study time also tended to have higher grades.

However, this does *not* mean that increasing study time will automatically increase a student’s grade by 2.4 points.

It only shows the pattern we observed in this dataset.

---

## Slide 6 – Previous Failures
*~40 seconds*

Previous academic difficulty was another important warning sign.

Students with *no previous failures* had an average final grade of about *12.5*.

Students with *one previous failure* averaged around *8.6*.

That is a large difference.

For parents and schools, the important message is not that failing once automatically causes another failure.

Instead, previous academic difficulty may help identify students who could benefit from support earlier.

---

## Slide 7 – Term Grades as an Early Warning
*~60 seconds*

This was one of the most useful findings in the whole analysis.

Students who were already *below the pass mark in term 1* were much more likely to fail the final grade.

Around *56%* of students who scored below 10 in term 1 later failed the year.

By comparison, only about *2%* of students who passed term 1 later failed the final grade.

And most importantly, *88% of students who eventually failed were already below the pass mark in term 1*.

For schools, this is very useful.

It means we do not necessarily need to wait until the end of the year to identify students who may need support.

Term 1 results can act as an early-warning signal.

At the same time, G1 and G2 are earlier academic grades and are naturally very close to the final grade, so they are useful for early warning, but not for explaining why students perform differently.

---

## Slide 8 – Other Factors
*~40 seconds*

We also found that some factors were less strongly related to final grades than we expected.

For example, absences had only a *very weak relationship* with final grades in this sample.

Students with fewer absences did have slightly higher average grades, but the overall relationship was weak.

This is an important reminder that something may seem obviously important, but the data may show a more limited relationship.

So schools should avoid making decisions based only on assumptions.

---

## Slide 9 – Findings That Surprised Us
*~65 seconds*

We also found several patterns that were less straightforward.

First, students who reported going out with friends *very rarely* and those who went out *very often* both had failure rates of around *25%*.

For students in the middle groups, the failure rate was closer to *11%*.

This does not tell us why the pattern exists, but it suggests the relationship is not simply “more social activity is better” or “less is better”.

Second, students whose parents lived together and those whose parents lived apart had very similar observed failure rates, at around *15% in both groups*.

Third, students receiving paid extra classes had a failure rate of around *23%, compared with **15%* among students without paid extra classes.

This does *not* mean tutoring makes students perform worse.

*One possible explanation* is that students who are already struggling may be more likely to receive tutoring.

The dataset cannot tell us which explanation is correct.

---

## Slide 10 – Looking at Several Factors Together
*~70 seconds*

Next, we looked at several factors together using a statistical model.

The purpose was not to perfectly predict each student’s future.

Instead, it helped us see whether certain patterns were still present when several factors were considered at the same time.

For example, students who reported wanting to go to university tended to have higher predicted grades.

Students reporting more study time also tended to have higher predicted grades.

On the other hand, previous failures and higher school-day drinking were linked with lower predicted grades.

But these numbers should not be treated as *guaranteed outcomes*.

For example, we cannot say that encouraging a student to want to go to university will automatically increase their grade.

Overall, the model explains about *one-third of the differences in final grades*.

So it contains useful information, but a large part of student performance is still unexplained.

---

## Slide 11 – What These Findings Mean for Families and Schools
*~65 seconds*

So what can families and schools take from these results?

For schools, the clearest message is to pay attention to *term 1 results*.

Students who are already struggling early in the year may benefit from support before problems become more serious.

Students with previous academic difficulties may also deserve earlier attention.

For families, the findings suggest that future goals, regular study habits and balanced routines are useful areas to discuss with students.

However, these should not be treated as guaranteed solutions.

The key idea is to use these patterns as *signals for support*, not as fixed rules.

And if schools introduce support programs, they should evaluate whether those programs actually improve student outcomes before applying them more widely.

---

## Slide 12 – Limitations
*~60 seconds*

There are several important limitations.

First, the data came from only *two secondary schools in Portugal*.

So we should not assume that the same patterns would apply directly to Australia or other populations.

Second, this is observational data.

We are looking at existing student information rather than running an experiment.

So we can identify relationships, but we cannot prove cause and effect.

Third, some groups in the dataset were quite small, and several variables were self-reported, which may introduce some uncertainty.

Also, G1 and G2 are earlier grades, so their very strong relationship with G3 is partly expected.

Finally, the model explains around *33% of the observed differences in grades, and an individual prediction can still be wrong by around **three points*.

So this should be used as a guide, not as a precise prediction system for individual students.

---

## Slide 13 – Responsible Use and Next Steps
*~55 seconds*

Responsible use of student data is also very important.

Schools should protect student privacy and avoid using statistical patterns to label students as “low ability” or “problem students”.

The purpose should be to support students, not judge them.

If similar research involved Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, it would also be important to respect *data sovereignty*.

In simple terms, this means communities should have authority and a meaningful say in how their data is collected, used and shared.

Our current dataset does *not* contain Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community data.

For future work, we would recommend testing these findings using more recent local data, following students over time, and evaluating whether support programs actually improve outcomes.

---

## Slide 14 – Conclusion
*~50 seconds*

To conclude, we have three main messages.

First, *warning signs often appear early*.

Many students who eventually failed were already below the pass mark in term 1.

Second, previous academic difficulty, study time and several lifestyle patterns were linked with final grades.

And third, these results should be used to *guide support, not predict or label a student’s future*.

The main value of this analysis is not to tell us exactly who will fail.

It is to help families and schools notice possible warning signs earlier and make more informed decisions about where support may be needed.
